\begin{abstract}
Checking a financial claim against a regulatory filing means reading a document many times
longer than a small model's context window, and passing every filing to a frontier model is
expensive. We build a verification pipeline that runs two small models on device and calls a
cloud model only when a disagreement gate or an arithmetic detector fires. On \nTest{}
held-out claims from FINDVER the routed pipeline scores \accRouted{}, compared with
\accCloud{} for a cloud-only baseline. The difference is not significant (paired McNemar,
\mcnemarAllDisagree{} disagreements, $p = \mcnemarAllP{}$), and the routed system spends
\costRatio{} of the cloud tokens, with \onDeviceRate{} of claims settled entirely on device.
That aggregate hides structure that matters in deployment, since the tie is a significant
loss on one subset offset by ties on the other two. The cause is that the gate reads
agreement between two models of the same family as confidence, when on that subset their
agreement is correlated error worth \keptLocalIEGap{} points. We also report a BM25
retriever that reaches \recallOursTest{} macro recall with no neural model and no API call,
\recallGainTest{} points above the strongest retriever published on this benchmark, and an
error taxonomy in which one failure mode accounts for \missingInfoKnowTest{} of
knowledge-subset errors.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Claims about a company's financial performance circulate faster than the documents that
would settle them, and settling one means reading a filing that runs to tens of thousands of
words. FINDVER \citep{zhao2024findver} formalises the task: given a claim and the SEC filing
it refers to, a system must decide whether the filing entails or refutes the claim. The
filings average roughly 41,000 words and interleave narrative text with dozens of tables.
The strongest published system, a frontier cloud model, reaches 77.2\% on them, whereas
human financial experts reach 93.3\%.

The obvious approach, sending every filing to a frontier model, fits this setting badly.
Filings under analysis are commercially sensitive before they are public, and a prompt on
this task is dominated by retrieved document text and not by the claim, so the per-token
price is paid on the document over and over. Whether a large model can verify a financial
claim is already settled, so the deployment question is how little of that model a working
system actually needs.

\paragraph{Goal.}
Our aim is to measure that quantity, not to argue for an architecture. We build a
verification pipeline whose default path runs entirely on the user's own hardware and which
consults a frontier cloud model only when a gate decides the local models should not be
trusted. Nothing is trained or fine-tuned. We then put two questions to the system. How
much accuracy does routing give up against sending everything to the cloud? And does the
aggregate answer to that question survive being broken apart?

To answer them in order: the routed pipeline ties the cloud-only baseline overall, and that
aggregate does not survive being broken apart. The second answer is the more informative of
the two. The tie is one significant loss and two ties, and the loss has a single
identifiable cause, which lies in how we built the gate and not in the benchmark.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\itemsep0pt
\item A routed edge-cloud pipeline that ties a frontier cloud model on \nTest{} held-out
claims at \costRatio{} of the cloud cost, with \onDeviceRate{} of claims answered without
the document leaving the device, and with no training at any stage
(Section~\ref{sec:headline}).
\item Evidence that correlated error \citep{kim2025correlated} is severe enough between two
models of one family to break a deployment gate that treats their agreement as confidence.
On the information-extraction subset that agreement costs \keptLocalIEGap{} points against a
model that would have answered those claims correctly (Section~\ref{sec:ie}).
\item A trigger that escalates arithmetic claims unconditionally. It beats a disagreement
gate on precisely the claims such a gate cannot see, because both local models fail them in
the same direction (Section~\ref{sec:triggers}).
\item An error taxonomy on the held-out split in which one failure mode, the model declaring
the evidence absent and refuting on that basis, accounts for \missingInfoKnowTest{} of
knowledge-subset errors and \missingInfoAllTest{} of all errors
(Section~\ref{sec:taxonomy}).
\item A retrieval result obtained with no model and no API call: a BM25 baseline reaching
\recallOursTest{} macro recall on the held-out split, \recallGainTest{} points above the
strongest retriever published on this benchmark (Section~\ref{sec:retrieval}). We keep it a
retrieval result and claim nothing further, because recall and answer quality are close to
decoupled in our pipeline.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Fit to this workshop.}
The system addresses efficient inference under real-world constraints. It is built for a
machine that cannot hold a frontier model, and we treat its cost, its escalation rate and
its latency as first-class results, not as an appendix. The analysis addresses benchmarks
and evaluation for real-world deployment. An aggregate accuracy can hide a significant
per-subset regression, and a confidence signal can mislead in a way that only a paired
comparison exposes. Both are failures of measurement, not of modelling, and the
single-number protocol this benchmark is normally reported under makes both invisible.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

FINDVER \citep{zhao2024findver} evaluates claim verification against SEC filings that run to
tens of thousands of tokens. It partitions its claims into three subsets according to
whether the decisive step is information extraction, numerical reasoning, or domain
knowledge. The documents exceed the context window of any model that fits on consumer
hardware, so the benchmark is normally approached under retrieval augmentation
\citep{lewis2020rag}, and the published baselines hold the retriever fixed so that systems
are compared on verification, not on search.

We found no other method evaluated on FINDVER besides MACE \citep{saha2026mace}, which
decomposes verification into three roles and assigns every role to the same large model.
MACE adopts the benchmark's retrieval configuration unchanged, stating that retrieval is not
its focus, and its smallest reported configuration needs roughly 27 billion parameters
resident. We keep the premise that verification benefits from separating roles, and we
pursue the question MACE leaves open: which model should perform each role, when the
available models differ by orders of magnitude in cost and in where they can be run.

Delegating computation to an interpreter instead of to the model is well established.
Where chain-of-thought prompting \citep{wei2022cot} asks the model to carry out the
arithmetic itself, Program of Thoughts \citep{chen2022pot} and PAL \citep{gao2022pal} hand
it to an interpreter,
and TART \citep{lu2025tart} applies it to tabular reasoning through a table formatter and a
code generator. TART fine-tunes every module and evaluates on single-table, closed-domain
data, so neither its training requirements nor its setting transfer here. We built the same
component for the arithmetic subset of this benchmark, and report in
Section~\ref{sec:sandbox} why it does not work at the model scale a consumer device allows.

% Citation gap CLOSED 27 August 2026. Twelve verified entries added to refs.bib.
Routing between a small local model and a large remote one is a familiar idea in deployment,
usually framed as a cascade in which a cheap model answers when it can and defers when it
cannot \citep{moslem2026routing}, and it is the form edge-cloud collaboration usually takes
\citep{li2025collaborative}. The deferral signal is the design question. It is often the
cheap model's own calibrated uncertainty \citep{kotte2026ucci}, and it is often agreement
across an ensemble, either as exact agreement between cascade stages
\citep{kolawole2025agreement} or as semantic agreement between open-ended outputs
\citep{soiffer2025semantic}. Agreement is attractive because it needs no training and no
access to logits, which is what led us to it. It also assumes the agreeing models fail
independently. That assumption is already known to be unsafe in aggregate: model errors
correlate strongly across the population of released models \citep{kim2025correlated}, and
a panel of nine frontier judges has been shown to carry roughly two independent votes
\citep{kohli2026judges}. We report what that costs a two-model deferral gate whose members
share a family, in Section~\ref{sec:ie}, and it is not uniform across the benchmark: the
signal is sound on two subsets and misleading on the third.

Financial claim verification has moved quickly in the year since FINDVER was published.
FinGround decomposes an answer into atomic claims, routes each by claim type to a matching
verification strategy including reconstruction of the arithmetic, and distils the detector
to 8B for deployment \citep{guo2026finground}. VeriFin grounds claims in XBRL data and
discharges them with a solver rather than a model \citep{hall2026verifin}. FinVerBench
examines whether models are calibrated when they verify financial statements at all
\citep{panda2026finverbench}. None of the three is evaluated on FINDVER, and none targets a
device that cannot hold a frontier model, which is the constraint that shapes our system.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

The pipeline verifies a claim in three stages, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}, and each
stage is assigned to the least expensive component that can perform it reliably. Retrieval
runs in plain Python and involves no model. Two small models hosted on the device then
produce independent verdicts. A frontier cloud model is consulted only when a gate
determines that the local pair should not be trusted, so the network is an exception and not
the default path. No part of the pipeline is trained or fine-tuned.

\subsection{Retrieval}
\label{sec:retrieval}

Reports in this benchmark are too long to fit in a prompt, so every system evaluated on it
retrieves first. Our retriever is BM25 \citep{robertson2009bm25} over the report's own
context elements. It runs no neural model and makes no API call. On the held-out split it
reaches \recallOursTest{} macro recall at $k{=}10$, against \recallEmbedTest{} for
\texttt{text-embedding-3-large}, the strongest retriever reported on this benchmark, and
\recallUpstreamBMTest{} for the benchmark's own BM25. Element recall is
\recallElementTest{}, and \recallAllGoldTest{} of claims receive every element they need.

The eleven point gap to the benchmark's own BM25 comes from implementation and not from
the algorithm, and the development split accounts for it. Lucene's IDF variant is worth
\ablationIDF{} points, and discarding punctuation tokens is worth a further
\ablationPunct{} points, while stemming contributes nothing. There is a mechanism behind
the punctuation result. Treating punctuation as tokens inflates measured element length by
\tableInflation{}$\times$ for tables against \paraInflation{}$\times$ for paragraphs, so
the length normalisation term penalises tables disproportionately and pushes them below
the retrieval cut, and tables constitute roughly 18\% of elements while carrying much of
this benchmark's evidence. We report this as a measurement and not as a design insight:
our tokenizer discards punctuation because it was reused from an evidence-checking
utility, and we identified the mechanism afterwards.

We tried three further upgrades, and none of them helped. Decomposing a claim into
sub-claims and merging the resulting rankings six different ways never surpassed querying
with the whole claim, and at matched candidate count the single query wins by 3.2 points.
Fusing a dense arm into the ranking gains nothing when the arm is left untuned, and a weak
arm is actively harmful, since reciprocal rank fusion grants every list an equal vote and
adding a retriever scoring 33\% costs 5.1 points. Sweeping the $k_1$ and $b$ parameters
moves the score only within its own noise.

We report these as retrieval results and nothing more. In our pipeline retrieval quality
and answer quality are close to decoupled. Supplying perfect evidence does not raise
accuracy ($p = 0.116$), removing a third of the evidence does not lower it ($p = 1.000$),
and raising $k$ from 10 to 20 gains 10.8 points of recall and no measurable accuracy at
87\% more prompt tokens. A recall gain on this benchmark should therefore not be read as a
verification gain.

\subsection{The local tier}
\label{sec:local}

Both local arms are Qwen2.5-Coder models served through Ollama, at 3 and 7 billion
parameters respectively, quantised to four bits and holding roughly 7.5\,GB resident when
both are loaded. They are given the same prompt, the same retrieved context at $k{=}10$, a
context window of 32,768 tokens, temperature zero and a fixed seed. Each produces a verdict
independently, and neither sees the other's output.

The two arms come from the same model family. We chose them for latency and output-format
compliance, not for independence, and that choice turns out to determine the system's
principal weakness. Section~\ref{sec:ie} treats it as a finding and not as an implementation
detail.

\subsection{The escalation gate}
\label{sec:gate}

A claim is sent to the cloud when either of two independent triggers fires, and the cloud
verdict then replaces the local one.

The first trigger is disagreement. When the two local arms return opposing verdicts, the
gate treats the claim as one the local tier cannot settle. The intuition is the
conventional one for a cascade, namely that concordance between two models indicates a
claim is easy and discordance indicates it is hard.

The second trigger is a detector for claims that assert a computed quantity. It operates
on the claim text alone, before any model is called, and consists of three regular
expressions that match a copula followed by a number at the end of a sentence, the word
\emph{percentage}, and a copula followed by a hedging adverb such as \emph{approximately}
or \emph{exactly}. On the held-out split the detector fires on \trigNumericN{} claims at
0.984 precision and 0.737 recall against the benchmark's own subset annotation, which it
never reads. The regularity it exploits is one of phrasing: numerical claims in this
benchmark are equations written as English sentences and average 20.5 words, whereas the
other two subsets embed their figures in narrative and average 41 words. We note that this
may be an artefact of how the claims were generated, and we do not claim the detector
would transfer to claims written by a human analyst.

The second trigger exists because the first cannot see a particular class of failure. Where
both local arms are wrong in the same way, they agree, and their agreement suppresses
escalation exactly where it is most needed. Section~\ref{sec:triggers} quantifies
how much each trigger contributes.

\subsection{Why arithmetic is escalated rather than executed}
\label{sec:sandbox}

Given that one subset of this benchmark turns on arithmetic, the natural design is to have
the model emit a calculation and an interpreter evaluate it, following Program of Thoughts
\citep{chen2022pot}. We built that component and measured it, and it does not work at this
model scale.

The component runs on claims the arithmetic detector has already routed to the cloud, so it
can remove work but never add any. The local model is asked for the operands, the operation
and the value the claim asserts, rather than for a verdict. An interpreter then performs the
calculation and compares the result to the claim at the precision the claim itself states,
which is a rule with no threshold to tune. It abstains, and the claim proceeds to the cloud,
whenever the operands it names do not appear in the retrieved text or the operation
contradicts the units of the claimed value.

On all \arithN{} arithmetic claims of the development split it answered \arithAnswered{} and
scored \arithAcc{}, against \arithCloudAcc{} for the cloud model on those same claims
(paired McNemar, \arithDisagree{} disagreements, $p = \arithP{}$). Output format was not the
difficulty: \arithFormatFail{} response of \arithN{} failed to produce the requested fields.
The failure is operand selection. A component that names the wrong figures computes a value
that cannot match the claim, and therefore always answers refuted; ours answered refuted
\arithSaidRefuted{} times against \arithSaidEntailed{} entailed, on a split that is half
each. Supplying the correct operands and leaving everything else unchanged raises the same
rule to \arithOracle{}, so the interpreter was never the bottleneck. Identifying which two of
several hundred figures in a retrieved passage a claim refers to is the bottleneck, and it is
the same weakness that made claim decomposition unusable in Section~\ref{sec:retrieval}.

The larger local model does not rescue the component. Running the same extraction on our 7B
arm raises it to \arithSevenBAcc{} on \arithSevenBAnswered{} answered claims, against
\arithSevenBCloudAcc{} for the cloud model on those same claims, still a significant loss
(\arithSevenBDisagree{} disagreements, $p = \arithP{}$). Roughly two and a half times the
parameters recovers a third of the gap and does not close it, and the larger model exhibits
the same bias towards refutation. We therefore report this as a limit of the local tier
rather than of one model in it, while noting that two points do not establish a trend.

We therefore route arithmetic claims to the cloud model instead of executing them, and
Section~\ref{sec:triggers} reports the escalation value this buys. We report the component
rather than omitting it, because the negative result locates a limit of small on-device models
that an accuracy table does not show.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Splits and protocol.}
FINDVER provides a development split of \nDev{} claims and a test split of \nTest{} claims,
both with labels. The benchmark ships no training data, and no component of this system has
trained parameters, so development consists of selecting components and settings rather than
fitting them. We carry out that selection on the development split, including the retriever,
the value of $k$, the choice of local models, the prompts and both gate triggers, and we
report all figures on the test split. Reruns of an identical configuration on this hardware
move roughly one verdict in ten, so we treat any difference smaller than that as noise rather
than as a result.

\paragraph{Scoring.}
A verdict is extracted from the model's response by a rule-based extractor that searches the
whole response, not only its final sentence, since small models frequently place the verdict
elsewhere. Responses from which no verdict can be recovered are counted in an explicit
unparseable category and scored as incorrect, which we call strict scoring. We report the
unparseable rate alongside every accuracy figure, because a system that fails to answer and
a system that answers wrongly are different failures and strict scoring alone conflates
them. On the held-out split the rate is \unparseThreeB{} for the 3B arm, \unparseSevenB{}
for the 7B, and \unparseRouted{} for the routed system. Where a figure of ours is placed
beside a published one, we additionally report it under the benchmark's own convention, in
which unrecoverable verdicts are resolved by a coin flip. Comparing a strictly scored number
with a coin-flipped one would understate ours by exactly the amount our models fail the
output format.

\paragraph{Hardware.}
Experiments were executed on a desktop with a consumer GPU, since a full run of the routed
pipeline over the test split takes about \latRunHours{} hours there. The device of record
for the deployment claim, however, is a 2017 laptop with 16\,GB of memory and no usable
GPU, and every latency figure must name its machine because the ordering reverses between
them. On the desktop the local 3B arm is the fastest of the three models at
\latThreeBMed{} seconds per claim, faster than either cloud model; on the laptop the same
arm takes roughly 420 seconds. Every latency figure in this paper comes from the desktop.
We have not measured the routed pipeline end to end on the laptop, and we do not report a
target-device latency we did not measure.

The deficit is confined to the claims the gate chose to keep. When we split FDV-IE by what
the gate did, the routed system scores \keptLocalIERouted{} on the \keptLocalIEn{} claims
kept on device, and the cloud model would have scored \keptLocalIECloud{} on those same
claims, a gap of \keptLocalIEGap{} points. On the \escalatedIEn{} claims that were
escalated it scores \escalatedIERouted{} against \escalatedIECloud{}, which puts it
slightly ahead. The gate is therefore not escalating too little in general, but instead
keeping the wrong claims.

This follows from the composition of the local tier. Our two local arms are the 3B and 7B
members of one model family, and they share a tokenizer, a training corpus and an
architecture. When two such models agree they are frequently agreeing on the same mistake,
and a disagreement gate cannot tell that apart from two independent models converging on a
correct answer. On FDV-IE the errors are concentrated in exactly those agreement cases, so
the gate reads correlated error as confidence and declines to escalate.

This identifies a design parameter that cascade work does not usually treat as one. The
value of a disagreement signal is bounded by the independence of the models producing it, so
the diversity of the local ensemble sets the ceiling on the gate before any threshold is
tuned. We assembled our tier on the grounds normally used, namely latency and output-format
compliance, and those grounds select for exactly the correlation that disables the signal.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} sets out the two components this specifies.

\subsection{Escalation earns its cost, and both triggers contribute}
\label{sec:triggers}

On the \cloudCalls{} claims where the cloud was called, the 7B would have scored
\escWouldSevenB{} and the 3B would have scored \escWouldThreeB{}. The cloud scored
\escCloudGave{}. Escalation therefore buys \escGain{} points on exactly the claims the
gate chose to pay for, and that is the quantity a cascade has to justify.

When we split by which trigger fired, the \trigDisagreeN{} claims escalated for
disagreement go from \trigDisagreeSevenB{} under the 7B to \trigDisagreeCloud{}, a gain of
\trigDisagreeGain{} points. The \trigNumericN{} claims escalated by the numeric detector
go from \trigNumericSevenB{} to \trigNumericCloud{}, a gain of \trigNumericGain{} points.
The two triggers are not redundant. The numeric detector fires on claims where both local
models agree and are wrong together, which a disagreement gate cannot see by construction,
and this measurement is what stands behind the design choice in Section~\ref{sec:gate}.

The gate is also selecting effectively among the claims it keeps. Claims kept on device
score \accKeptLocal{}, on a local tier whose all-claim accuracy is \accSevenB{}, so the gate
is retaining the claims the local models handle well. Section~\ref{sec:ie} shows where that
judgement fails.

\subsection{An error taxonomy, and a label bias that replicates}
\label{sec:taxonomy}

One failure mode dominates the error budget. The model states that the document does not
contain the information needed, refutes the claim on that basis, and is wrong. On the
held-out split \missingInfoCiteRateTest{} of knowledge-subset responses cite missing
information, 98.4\% of those then refute, and \missingInfoWrongTest{} of those refutations
are incorrect against a base rate of \missingInfoBaseTest{} for refutations in general.
That single category accounts for \missingInfoKnowTest{} of all knowledge-subset errors and
\missingInfoAllTest{} of every error the system makes, and it replicates at nearly identical
size on the development split (\missingInfoKnowDev{}). The category is matched by an
anchored phrase family over model prose, and we spot-checked it by reading responses on the
development split.

Underlying it is a label bias that has now replicated on every configuration we have run.
Gold labels are \saysEntailedGold{} entailed, but the cloud model answers entailed on only
\saysEntailedCloud{} of claims, and the 3B answers entailed on only \saysEntailedThreeB{}.
Routing partly corrects this, at \saysEntailedRouted{}, and the routed system beats the
cloud baseline on entailed claims by 5.4 points (\entailedRouted{} against
\entailedCloud{}) while losing 8.5 points on refuted ones. Models on this task are biased
toward refutation, and the most likely reason is the one above: when the evidence is not
found, refuting is the cheaper answer.

Finally, we report an upper bound. Perfect selection among the verdicts our two local
models already produce, with no cloud call at all, would score \oracleLocalOnly{}
(\oracleLocalOnlyci{}), above the cloud baseline. Adding the cloud verdict to that
selection gives \oracleThreeWay{}. Neither is implementable, since both require the gold
label in order to make the selection. They bound how much of the remaining gap is a gating
problem and not a model problem, and most of the gap falls on the gating side.

\section{Conclusion and future work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A verification pipeline whose default path runs on consumer hardware ties a frontier cloud
model on \nTest{} held-out financial claims, at \costRatio{} of the cost, with
\onDeviceRate{} of filings never leaving the device and with no training at any stage.
That result holds in aggregate, and the second half of this paper shows that the aggregate
has structure: the tie is a loss on one subset and ties elsewhere, with a single cause that
points directly at what to build next.

That cause is specific enough to act on. Our gate treats agreement between two small
models as evidence that a claim is easy, and because both models come from one family, their
agreement on the information-extraction subset is correlated error worth \keptLocalIEGap{}
points on the claims the gate keeps. Two components follow from this. The local ensemble
should be diverse by construction, since two models from different families, or one model
queried under materially different conditions, would give the gate a signal that agreement
does not currently carry. The subset where the signal fails is also identifiable in
advance from the claim text, in the same way the arithmetic trigger already is, so a
targeted trigger can escalate the claims the disagreement signal cannot see. Both are
measurable against the development split without re-reading the held-out one.

Three further directions come out of the measurements above. The escalation triggers are
hand-written, and the taxonomy in Section~\ref{sec:taxonomy} suggests a learned or
pattern-derived trigger aimed at the specific failure that dominates the error budget, which
the present gate does not detect at all. A trigger of that kind is one half of an agentic
skill in the sense now being formalised \citep{jiang2026skills}, a reusable procedure
carrying its own applicability condition; the other half, the procedure itself, is where the
interpreter we did not build would sit. The oracle bound indicates that most of the
remaining gap is a selection problem and not a capability problem, so a better gate is a
better investment than a better local model. Every latency figure here comes from a desktop,
so measuring the pipeline end to end on the target laptop is necessary before any claim
about deployability on that class of hardware, and we make none here.

\paragraph{One benchmark, one document type.}
Every result here comes from FINDVER, which is built on English-language SEC filings.
Neither the retriever's advantage nor the numeric detector's precision should be assumed
to transfer to another document class, and the detector in particular exploits a
regularity of phrasing that may be an artefact of how these claims were written.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.}
The cloud endpoint does not honour a temperature or a seed, so its verdicts vary between
identical runs. Our own local models are seeded at temperature zero and still move roughly
one verdict in ten between reruns on the same machine. Comparisons in this paper are
therefore paired wherever a paired test is available.

\paragraph{Latency is not measured on the target device.}
All timings come from the GPU desktop. The laptop that motivates the on-device framing has
been measured for a single local model only, and the routed pipeline has not been run on
it end to end.
