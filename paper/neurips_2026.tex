\begin{abstract}
Verifying a financial claim against a regulatory filing requires reading a document far
longer than a small model's context window, and sending every filing to a frontier model
is expensive. We build a claim verification pipeline that runs two small models on device
and calls a cloud model only when a disagreement gate or an arithmetic detector fires. On
\nTest{} held-out claims from FINDVER the routed pipeline scores \accRouted{} against a
cloud-only baseline's \accCloud{}. The difference is not significant (paired McNemar,
\mcnemarAllDisagree{} disagreements, $p = \mcnemarAllP{}$), and the routed system uses
\costRatio{} of the cloud tokens with \onDeviceRate{} of claims answered entirely on
device. That aggregate hides structure that matters to anyone deploying it. The tie is a
significant loss on one subset offset by ties on the others, and we identify the cause:
the gate reads agreement between two models of the same family as confidence, and on that
subset the agreement is correlated error worth \keptLocalIEGap{} points. We report a BM25
retriever that reaches \recallOursTest{} macro recall with no neural model and no API
call, \recallGainTest{} points above the strongest retriever published on this benchmark,
and an error taxonomy in which one failure mode accounts for \missingInfoKnowTest{} of
knowledge-subset errors.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Claims about a company's financial performance circulate faster than the documents that
would settle them, and settling one means reading a filing that runs to tens of thousands
of words. The FINDVER benchmark \citep{zhao2024findver} formalises this task. Given a claim
and the SEC filing it refers to, a system must decide whether the filing entails or refutes
the claim. The filings average roughly 41,000 words and interleave narrative text with
dozens of tables, and the strongest published system, a frontier cloud model, reaches
77.2\% where human financial experts reach 93.3\%.

Sending every filing to a frontier model is the obvious way to attack this, and it is the
wrong shape for the setting. Filings under analysis are commercially sensitive before they
are public, and a prompt on this task is dominated by retrieved document text rather than
by the claim, so the per-token price is paid on the document over and over. The deployment
question is therefore not whether a large model can verify a financial claim, which is
already settled, but how little of that model a working system actually needs.

\paragraph{Goal.}
We set out to measure that quantity rather than to argue for an architecture. We build a
verification pipeline whose default path runs entirely on the user's own hardware, and
which consults a frontier cloud model only when a gate decides that the local models should
not be trusted. No component is trained or fine-tuned, every design decision was fixed on a
development split, and the reported split was read once. We then ask two questions of it.
How much accuracy does the routed system give up against sending everything to the cloud,
and does the aggregate answer to that question survive being broken apart.

It does not, and that is the more useful half of the result. The routed pipeline ties the
cloud-only baseline overall, and the tie decomposes into one significant loss and two ties.
The loss has a single identifiable cause, and the cause is a property of how the gate was
built rather than a property of the benchmark.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\itemsep0pt
\item A routed edge-cloud pipeline that ties a frontier cloud model on \nTest{} held-out
claims at \costRatio{} of the cloud cost, with \onDeviceRate{} of claims answered without
the document leaving the device, and with no training of any kind
(Section~\ref{sec:headline}).
\item Evidence that agreement between two models of the same family is correlated error
rather than independent confirmation. Our gate reads that agreement as confidence, and on
the information-extraction subset it costs \keptLocalIEGap{} points against a model that
would have answered those claims correctly (Section~\ref{sec:ie}).
\item A trigger that escalates arithmetic claims unconditionally, which beats a
disagreement gate on exactly the claims a disagreement gate cannot see, because both local
models fail them in the same direction (Section~\ref{sec:triggers}).
\item An error taxonomy on the held-out split in which one failure mode, the model
declaring the evidence absent and refuting on that basis, accounts for
\missingInfoKnowTest{} of knowledge-subset errors and \missingInfoAllTest{} of all errors
(Section~\ref{sec:taxonomy}).
\item A retrieval result obtained with no model and no API call: a BM25 baseline reaching
\recallOursTest{} macro recall on the held-out split, \recallGainTest{} points above the
strongest retriever published on this benchmark (Section~\ref{sec:retrieval}). We report it
as a retrieval result, because in our pipeline recall and answer quality are close to
decoupled.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Fit to this workshop.}
The system addresses efficient inference under real-world constraints: it is built for a
machine that cannot hold a frontier model, and its cost, its escalation rate and its
latency are reported as first-class results rather than as an appendix. The analysis
addresses benchmarks and evaluation for real-world deployment. An aggregate accuracy that
hides a significant per-subset regression, and a confidence signal that is misleading in a
way only a paired comparison exposes, are both failures of measurement rather than of
modelling, and both are invisible to the single-number protocol this benchmark is normally
reported under.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

FINDVER \citep{zhao2024findver} evaluates claim verification against SEC filings that run to
tens of thousands of tokens, and partitions its claims into three subsets according to
whether the decisive step is information extraction, numerical reasoning, or domain
knowledge. Because the documents exceed the context window of any model that fits on
consumer hardware, the benchmark is normally approached under retrieval augmentation, and
the published baselines hold the retriever fixed so that systems are compared on
verification rather than on search.

We found no other method evaluated on FINDVER besides MACE \citep{saha2026mace}, which
decomposes verification into three roles and assigns every role to the same large model.
MACE adopts the benchmark's retrieval configuration unchanged, stating that retrieval is
not its focus, and its smallest reported configuration requires roughly 27 billion
parameters resident. We retain the premise that verification benefits from separating
roles, and pursue the question MACE does not ask: which model should perform each role
when the available models differ by orders of magnitude in cost and in where they can be
run.

Delegating computation to an interpreter rather than to the model is well established.
Program of Thoughts \citep{chen2022pot} and PAL \citep{gao2022pal} introduced the
technique, and TART \citep{lu2025tart} applies it to tabular reasoning through a table
formatter and a code generator. TART fine-tunes every module and evaluates on
single-table, closed-domain data, so neither its training requirements nor its setting
transfer here. We treated code execution as the obvious remedy for the arithmetic subset,
measured what it would be worth, and report in Section~\ref{sec:sandbox} why we did not
build it.

Routing between a small local model and a large remote one is a familiar idea in
deployment, usually framed as a cascade in which a cheap model answers when it can and
defers when it cannot. What that literature generally assumes, and what we find does not
hold here, is that the cheap model's own confidence is informative about when to defer.
Our results in Section~\ref{sec:results} show that a confidence signal built from
agreement between two related small models is systematically misleading on one class of
claims.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

The pipeline verifies a claim in three stages, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}, and each
stage is assigned to the least expensive component that can perform it reliably. Retrieval
runs in plain Python and involves no model. Two small models hosted on the device then
produce independent verdicts. A frontier cloud model is consulted only when a gate
determines that the local pair should not be trusted, so the network is used as an
exception rather than as the default path. No component is trained or fine-tuned at any
point.

\subsection{Retrieval}
\label{sec:retrieval}

Reports in this benchmark are too long to fit in a prompt, so every system evaluated on
it retrieves first. Our retriever is BM25 \citep{robertson2009bm25} over the report's own
context elements. It runs no neural model and makes no API call. On the held-out split it
reaches \recallOursTest{} macro recall at $k{=}10$, against \recallEmbedTest{} for
\texttt{text-embedding-3-large}, the strongest retriever reported on this benchmark, and
\recallUpstreamBMTest{} for the benchmark's own BM25. Element recall is
\recallElementTest{} and \recallAllGoldTest{} of claims receive every element they need.

The eleven point gap to the benchmark's own BM25 is a matter of implementation rather than
of algorithm, and it can be accounted for on the development split. Lucene's IDF variant
is worth \ablationIDF{} points and discarding punctuation tokens \ablationPunct{}, while
stemming contributes nothing. The punctuation result has a mechanism behind it. Treating
punctuation as tokens inflates measured element length by \tableInflation{}$\times$ for
tables against \paraInflation{}$\times$ for paragraphs, so the length normalisation term
penalises tables disproportionately and pushes them below the retrieval cut, and tables
constitute roughly 18\% of elements while carrying much of this benchmark's evidence. We
report this as a measurement rather than as a design insight, since our tokenizer discards
punctuation because it was reused from an evidence-checking utility and the mechanism was
identified afterwards.

Three upgrades that we expected to help did not. Decomposing a claim into sub-claims and
merging the resulting rankings six different ways never surpassed querying with the whole
claim, and at matched candidate count the single query wins by 3.2 points. Fusing a dense
arm into the ranking gains nothing untuned, and a weak arm is actively harmful, since
reciprocal rank fusion grants every list an equal vote and adding a retriever scoring 33\%
costs 5.1 points. Sweeping the $k_1$ and $b$ parameters moves the score only within its own
noise.

We report these as retrieval results and nothing more. In our pipeline retrieval quality
and answer quality are close to decoupled. Supplying perfect evidence does not raise
accuracy ($p = 0.116$), removing a third of the evidence does not lower it ($p = 1.000$),
and raising $k$ from 10 to 20 buys 10.8 points of recall and no measurable accuracy at
87\% more prompt tokens. A recall gain on this benchmark should therefore not be assumed
to constitute a verification gain.

\subsection{The local tier}
\label{sec:local}

Both local arms are Qwen2.5-Coder models served through Ollama, at 3 and 7 billion
parameters respectively, quantised to four bits and holding roughly 7.5\,GB resident when
both are loaded. They are given the same prompt, the same retrieved context at $k{=}10$, a
context window of 32,768 tokens, temperature zero and a fixed seed. Each produces a verdict
independently, and neither sees the other's output.

The two arms are drawn from the same model family, which was a decision made on grounds of
latency and output-format compliance rather than on grounds of independence. That choice
turns out to determine the system's principal weakness, and Section~\ref{sec:ie}
treats it as a finding rather than as an implementation detail.

\subsection{The escalation gate}
\label{sec:gate}

A claim is sent to the cloud when either of two independent triggers fires, and the cloud
verdict then replaces the local one.

The first trigger is disagreement. When the two local arms return opposing verdicts, the
gate treats the claim as one the local tier cannot settle. The intuition is the
conventional one for a cascade, namely that concordance between two models indicates a
claim is easy and discordance indicates it is hard.

The second trigger is a detector for claims that assert a computed quantity. It operates on
the claim text alone, before any model is called, and consists of three regular expressions
that match a copula followed by a number at the end of a sentence, the word
\emph{percentage}, and a copula followed by a hedging adverb such as \emph{approximately}
or \emph{exactly}. On the held-out split the detector fires on \trigNumericN{} claims at
0.984 precision and 0.737 recall against the benchmark's own subset annotation, which it
never reads. The regularity it exploits is one of phrasing: numerical claims in this
benchmark are equations written as English sentences and average 20.5 words, whereas the
other two subsets embed their figures in narrative and average 41. We note that this may be
an artefact of how the claims were generated, and we do not claim the detector would
transfer to claims written by a human analyst.

The second trigger exists because the first cannot see a particular class of failure.
Where both local arms are wrong in the same way, they agree, and agreement suppresses
escalation precisely when escalation is most needed. Section~\ref{sec:triggers} quantifies
how much each trigger contributes.

\subsection{Why arithmetic is escalated rather than executed}
\label{sec:sandbox}

Given that one subset of this benchmark turns on arithmetic, the natural design is to have
the model emit a calculation and an interpreter evaluate it, following Program of Thoughts
\citep{chen2022pot}. We measured the value of that component before building it, and the
measurement did not support it.

Three findings weighed against it. The failure that motivated the component was a
transcription error rather than a computation error, so an interpreter given the same
mis-transcribed operand returns the same wrong result. Arithmetic performed in prose
already yields the correct value on 64.4\% of numerical claims, leaving considerably less
headroom than assumed. Most decisively, producing the correct value barely predicts
producing the correct verdict: the two coincide on 34.8\% of claims against 41\% expected
under independence, and 73 claims reach the correct verdict without the correct value ever
appearing. The magnitude error the component was conceived to eliminate occurs on 8 of 250
responses.

We therefore route arithmetic claims to the cloud model rather than executing them, and we
report the escalation value this buys in Section~\ref{sec:triggers}.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Splits and protocol.}
FINDVER provides a development split of \nDev{} claims and a test split of \nTest{} claims,
both with labels. Every design decision in this system, including the retriever, the value
of $k$, the choice of local models, the prompts and both gate triggers, was made against
the development split. The test split was read once, at the end, to produce the numbers
reported here. We adopt this protocol because a prompt or a threshold iterated against the
same claims it is later evaluated on produces gains that cannot be distinguished from
noise, and reruns of an identical configuration on this hardware already move roughly one
verdict in ten. The protocol protects the reported figures; it does not undo the fact that
the pipeline as a whole was shaped on the development split, and we state that limitation
rather than implying otherwise.

\paragraph{Scoring.}
A verdict is extracted from the model's response by a rule-based extractor that searches
the entire response rather than only its final sentence, since small models frequently
place the verdict elsewhere. Responses from which no verdict can be recovered are counted
in an explicit unparseable category and scored as incorrect, which we refer to as strict
scoring. We report the unparseable rate alongside every accuracy figure, because a system
that fails to answer and a system that answers wrongly are different failures and strict
scoring alone conflates them. On the held-out split the rate is \unparseThreeB{} for the
3B arm, \unparseSevenB{} for the 7B, and \unparseRouted{} for the routed system. Where a
figure of ours is placed beside a published one we additionally report it under the
benchmark's own convention, in which unrecoverable verdicts are resolved by a coin flip,
since comparing a strictly scored number with a coin-flipped one understates ours by
exactly the amount our models fail the output format.

\paragraph{Hardware.}
Experiments were executed on a desktop with a consumer GPU, since a full run of the routed
pipeline over the test split takes about \latRunHours{} hours there. The device of record
for the deployment claim, however, is a 2017 laptop with 16\,GB of memory and no usable
GPU, and every latency figure must name its machine because the ordering reverses between
them. On the desktop the local 3B arm is the fastest of the three models at
\latThreeBMed{} seconds per claim, faster than either cloud model; on the laptop the same
arm takes roughly 420 seconds. Every latency figure in this paper is the desktop. We have
not measured the routed pipeline end to end on the laptop, and we do not report a
target-device latency we did not measure.

\label{sec:ie}

The deficit is located entirely in the claims the gate chose to keep. Splitting FDV-IE by
what the gate did, on the \keptLocalIEn{} claims kept on device the routed system scores
\keptLocalIERouted{} where the cloud model would have scored \keptLocalIECloud{}, a gap of
\keptLocalIEGap{} points. On the \escalatedIEn{} claims that were escalated it scores
\escalatedIERouted{} against \escalatedIECloud{}, slightly ahead. The gate is not
escalating too little in general. It is keeping the wrong claims.

The mechanism is the local tier's composition. Our two local arms are the 3B and 7B members
of one model family, and they share a tokenizer, a training corpus and an architecture.
When two such models agree they are frequently agreeing on the same mistake, and a
disagreement gate cannot distinguish that from two independent models converging on a
correct answer. On FDV-IE this is where the errors are: the gate reads correlated error as
confidence and declines to escalate. We chose a single family for latency and for
output-format compliance, and we did not anticipate that the choice would set the ceiling on
the gate. The implication for anyone building a cascade of this shape is that the diversity
of the local ensemble is a first-order design parameter, not an implementation detail.

\subsection{Escalation earns its cost, and both triggers contribute}
\label{sec:triggers}

On the \cloudCalls{} claims where the cloud was called, the 7B would have scored
\escWouldSevenB{} and the 3B \escWouldThreeB{}. The cloud scored \escCloudGave{}. Escalation
therefore buys \escGain{} points on exactly the claims the gate chose to pay for, which is
the quantity a cascade has to justify.

Splitting by which trigger fired, the \trigDisagreeN{} claims escalated for disagreement go
from \trigDisagreeSevenB{} under the 7B to \trigDisagreeCloud{}, a gain of
\trigDisagreeGain{} points. The \trigNumericN{} claims escalated by the numeric detector go
from \trigNumericSevenB{} to \trigNumericCloud{}, a gain of \trigNumericGain{}. The two
triggers are not redundant. The numeric detector fires on claims where both local models
agree and are wrong together, which is by construction invisible to a disagreement gate,
and this is the direct measurement behind the design choice in Section~\ref{sec:gate}.

The gate is also doing useful work on the claims it keeps. Claims kept on device score
\accKeptLocal{}, on a local tier whose all-claim accuracy is \accSevenB{}. The gate is
retaining the claims the local models handle well; Section~\ref{sec:ie} shows where that
judgement fails.

It is slower, and we report that rather than omitting it. Both local models run on every
claim before the gate can compare them, so a routed claim costs the 3B and the 7B in series,
\latThreeBMed{} and \latSevenBMed{} seconds respectively, before any cloud call. A single
cloud call takes \latCloudStageMed{} seconds. The median routed claim therefore takes
\latRoutedMed{} seconds against \latCloudMed{} for the cloud-only baseline. Local inference
on this hardware is not faster than a remote call to a well-provisioned endpoint, and a
cascade of this shape should not be sold on latency. The one latency property that does
favour it is the tail on claims that stay local, where the 95th percentile is
\latKeptLocalPFive{} seconds against \latCloudPFive{} for the cloud baseline, because no
network round trip is involved. Predictability, not speed.

\subsection{An error taxonomy, and a label bias that replicates}
\label{sec:taxonomy}

One failure mode dominates the error budget. The model states that the document does not
contain the information needed, refutes the claim on that basis, and is wrong. On the
held-out split \missingInfoCiteRateTest{} of knowledge-subset responses cite missing
information, 98.4\% of those then refute, and \missingInfoWrongTest{} of those refutations
are incorrect against a base rate of \missingInfoBaseTest{} for refutations in general.
That single category accounts for \missingInfoKnowTest{} of all knowledge-subset errors and
\missingInfoAllTest{} of every error the system makes, and it replicates at nearly identical
size on the development split (\missingInfoKnowDev{}). The category is matched by an
anchored phrase family over model prose, spot-checked by reading on the development split.

Underlying it is a label bias that has now replicated on every configuration we have run.
Gold labels are \saysEntailedGold{} entailed, but the cloud model answers entailed on only
\saysEntailedCloud{} of claims and the 3B on \saysEntailedThreeB{}. Routing partly corrects
this, at \saysEntailedRouted{}, and the routed system beats the cloud baseline on entailed
claims by 5.4 points (\entailedRouted{} against \entailedCloud{}) while losing 8.5 on
refuted ones. Models on this task are biased toward refutation, and the most likely reason
is the one above: when the evidence is not found, refuting is the cheaper answer.

Finally, an upper bound. Perfect selection among the verdicts our two local models already
produce, with no cloud call at all, would score \oracleLocalOnly{}
(\oracleLocalOnlyci{}), above the cloud baseline. Adding the cloud verdict to that selection
gives \oracleThreeWay{}. Neither is implementable, since both require the gold label to
choose; they bound how much of the remaining gap is a gating problem rather than a model
problem, and the answer is most of it.

\section{Conclusion and future work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A verification pipeline whose default path runs on consumer hardware ties a frontier cloud
model on \nTest{} held-out financial claims, at \costRatio{} of the cost, with
\onDeviceRate{} of filings never leaving the device and with no training of any kind. That
result is worth stating and it is not worth over-stating, which is why the second half of
this paper is an account of where it fails and why.

The failure is specific and it is ours rather than the benchmark's. Our gate treats
agreement between two small models as evidence that a claim is easy, and because both models
come from one family, their agreement on the information-extraction subset is correlated
error. It costs \keptLocalIEGap{} points on the claims the gate keeps. The immediate
consequence for future work is that the local ensemble should be diverse by construction:
two models from different families, or one model queried under materially different
conditions, would give the gate a signal that agreement currently does not carry.

Three further directions follow directly from the measurements above. The escalation
triggers are hand-written, and Section~\ref{sec:taxonomy}'s taxonomy suggests a learned or
pattern-derived trigger targeting the specific failure that dominates the error budget,
which the present gate does not detect at all. The oracle bound indicates that most of the
remaining gap is a selection problem rather than a capability problem, so a better gate is a
better investment than a better local model. And every latency figure here comes from a
desktop; measuring the pipeline end to end on the target laptop is necessary before any
claim about deployability on that class of hardware, and we do not make one here.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{The weakness we report was found on the reported split.}
The FDV-IE deficit and its mechanism were identified by reading the output of the held-out
run. No parameter was changed in response, and the numbers in this paper come from a
configuration frozen before that split was read, so the reported figures are unaffected. But
we would not have known where to look without it, and a future system that fixes this
weakness cannot claim the same protocol.

\paragraph{One benchmark, one document type.}
Every result here is FINDVER, which is English-language SEC filings. Neither the retriever's
advantage nor the numeric detector's precision should be assumed to transfer to another
document class, and the detector in particular exploits a regularity of phrasing that may be
an artefact of how these claims were written.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.}
The cloud endpoint does not honour a temperature or a seed, so its verdicts vary between
identical runs. Our own local models are seeded at temperature zero and still move roughly
one verdict in ten between reruns on the same machine. Comparisons in this paper are
therefore paired wherever a paired test is available.

\paragraph{Latency is not measured on the target device.}
All timings are the GPU desktop. The laptop that motivates the on-device framing has been
measured for a single local model only, and the routed pipeline has not been run on it end
to end.
